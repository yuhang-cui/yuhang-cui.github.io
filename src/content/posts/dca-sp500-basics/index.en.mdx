> **What this post covers**: where the S&P 500 came from, and how SPX, SPY, VOO and ES each relate to it.
>
> **Put simply**: SPX is a number; SPY and VOO actually buy the S&P 500 basket and package it into a stock fund you can buy; ES is a futures contract (with leverage) built on that number.

## Summary

<div class="summary">

**The S&P 500 is a score for “a basket of stocks”:**
S&P picks roughly 500 large American companies, weights them by market value and computes one number every day. At bottom it is a string of numbers, not a product you can buy or sell.

**Born in 1957:**
The S&P 500 has two advantages over the Dow: weighted by market value, and enough companies. That is why the market uses it as the yardstick, not the Dow.

**SPX runs 155 years, from 1871 to today, but the S&P 500 itself is only 69 years old:**
Everything before 1957 is spliced on: 1926–1957 uses its predecessor, the "S&P 90"; earlier than that is a monthly series the Cowles Commission reconstructed in the 1930s.
<span class="sub">Why anyone bothered: Alfred Cowles wanted to know whether stock forecasters were any good, and needed a "market" curve to compare them against, so he computed one back to 1871. S&P later attached it to the front of its own index, and Shiller extended it to today. So the first 86 years of SPX are stitched-together research data, not trading records.</span>

<details class="rc fold">
  <summary>SPY, VOO and ES</summary>
  <div class="rc-panel rc-def">

**SPY and VOO are two brands of the same thing:**
Both are funds. They actually buy these 500 companies' shares in proportion, then slice the whole into units and sell them to you. One share gives you a small piece of all 500 companies.<br />
A. SPY is older and has the highest volume, so it suits trading.<br />
B. VOO charges a third of the fee and is the better choice for holding long term.

**ES is a futures contract:**
Leverage, expiry dates, nearly 24-hour trading, no dividends. It is a day-trading tool.

## 1. What the S&P 500 is

<SideNote title="Who is S&P">
<Fragment slot="note">

Short for Standard & Poor's. It started in 1860 publishing financial manuals on railroad companies; later its main business became credit ratings on bonds, the AAA and BBB grades.

Why build an index: it already had financial data on the whole market. Computing a "market average" gave newspapers and fund managers a common yardstick. The more the index was quoted, the bigger the name; today SPY and VOO pay it a licence fee every year to use the name.

In 2012 the index business merged with Dow Jones and is now called S&P Dow Jones Indices.

</Fragment>

Think of it as “**a basket of stocks**”: S&P (S&P Dow Jones Indices) picks roughly 500 large American companies and puts them in the basket, each with a share set by its weight, bigger company bigger share, and uses their live share prices to compute one number, say 6,800 points.

</SideNote>

Think of the number as "the average share price of 500 large American companies". When it rises 1%, those companies rose 1% on average. What you can actually buy are the products built around this number, covered in section 3.

### How a company gets into the S&P 500

It is not “the 500 largest” automatically. A committee picks, against a few hard rules.

<details class="rc fold">
  <summary>The rules for getting in</summary>
  <div class="rc-panel rc-def">

<div class="exp-table">

| Rule | Detail |
|:---|:---|
| Domicile | A US company |
| Size | Above a threshold (about $22.7 billion from 2025, adjusted with the market) |
| Float | At least half the shares freely traded |
| Profit | Positive earnings in the latest quarter and over the latest four quarters combined |
| Liquidity | Enough trading volume |

</div>

<p class="table-note">The profit rule is why Tesla only got in at the end of 2020. Because Alphabet, Berkshire and others have two share classes, the index actually holds 503 tickers but is still called the 500.</p>

### How the weights work

Weighted by **free-float market cap**: a company's market value × the share of its stock that trades freely. Apple, Microsoft and Nvidia each take 6–7% on their own; the few hundred at the bottom take less than 1% each, some less than 0.1%. So when you buy the S&P 500, more than half the money goes to the top few dozen companies.

Compare the better-known Dow: only 30 companies, and **weighted by share price**. A company with a $500 share price carries ten times the weight of one at $50, regardless of how big the companies are. That is a leftover from hand calculation in 1896. It makes little sense today, but the name is too famous to drop.

## 2. A short history

<SideNote title="Buffett's ten-year bet">
<Fragment slot="note">

**The bet**: at the start of 2008 Buffett bet hedge-fund manager Ted Seides that over ten years the plainest S&P 500 index fund would beat a hand-picked portfolio of 5 funds of hedge funds (over 200 hedge funds underneath). $1 million at stake, the winner's charity gets it.

**The ride**: in 2008, year one, the index fell 37% and all 5 funds beat it. For the next nine years the index fund led almost every year.

**The result**: by the end of 2017 the index fund was up 125.8%, 8.5% a year. The 5 funds were up 21.7%, 42.3%, 87.7%, 2.8% and 27.0%, about 3% a year on average. Seides conceded in May 2017, before time was up.

**Why**: hedge funds charge "2% a year plus 20% of profits". Over ten years most of the gains went to the managers; an index fund charges a few hundredths of a percent.

Buffett later published his will in a shareholder letter: <u>90% of the money left to his wife goes into an S&P 500 index fund</u>.

</Fragment>

A few things worth one more sentence:

**Why 500.** Before 1957, 90 companies was about the limit of what people could compute by hand. That year S&P used an electronic computer for the first time (a Burroughs Datatron), which could run 500 companies in an hour, so the index jumped straight to 500. Every change since has swapped constituents; the count never moved.

**Why market-cap weighting.** This is the essential difference between the S&P 500 and the Dow. Weighting by market value means <u>“the proportions in which all the money in the market is invested across these 500 companies are your proportions”</u>. You do not guess which one will go up; you just follow the average holding of all investors. The idea became the standard for indexes worldwide, and it is where “buying the index is buying the whole market” comes from.

**The starting point.** Around 44 points at launch in 1957 (base period 1941–43 = 10). Today it is over 6,000: more than 100× without dividends, and roughly 1,000× with dividends reinvested. Those two numbers differ by a factor of 10.

### SPX: the index itself

Just a number. Its job is to be the **yardstick**: every product is judged against it.

### SPY: the first and most traded S&P ETF

Launched by State Street in 1993, the first ETF in the US. One share of SPY is a proportional slice of all 500 companies.

Its strengths are **the highest volume in the world and the most active options**, which makes it the traders' default. But it uses a 1993-era unit-trust structure: dividends it receives have to sit in cash until the quarterly payout and cannot be reinvested in between, and it cannot lend out its shares for extra income. Both make it a touch slower than VOO.

### VOO: Vanguard's S&P ETF

<SideNote title="VOO vs IVV">
<Fragment slot="note">

**Fee**: 0.03% for both.

**Structure**: both are modern open-end ETFs. Both can reinvest dividends inside the fund and lend out shares for a little extra. SPY can do neither.

**Origins**: IVV is BlackRock's, launched in 2000; VOO is Vanguard's, launched in 2010. VOO holds about $800 billion, IVV about $670 billion.

**Verdict**: same holdings, same fee, and the long-run return gap is on the order of a hundredth of a percent a year. Pick whichever your broker trades commission-free. Owning both is pointless.

</Fragment>

Launched in 2010, tracking the same basket, with holdings almost identical to SPY. The fee is 0.03%, a third of SPY's, and the structure is more modern: dividends can be reinvested straight away.

</SideNote>

SPY and VOO track the same thing, but **SPY suits options trading and VOO suits buying every month and holding for the long run**: a 0.06% fee gap compounds into a real difference over decades.

There is also IVV, BlackRock's version, with the same 0.03% fee as VOO. VOO, IVV and SPY are known as the “three S&P brothers”: the same holdings, with small differences in fee and structure.

### ES: S&P 500 futures

ES is a trader's tool; you do not need it for monthly buying. Skip this section if you like.

<details class="rc fold">
  <summary>What ES is</summary>
  <div class="rc-panel rc-def">

The E-mini contract CME launched in 1997. It is **not “buying 500 companies”**. It is a contract that settles wins and losses on the S&P's points: for every point the S&P rises, the long side gains $50 and the short side loses $50, and the other way round when it falls.

It differs from the other three in kind:

- **Leverage**: put down a few tens of thousands of dollars and your gains and losses are counted on about $340,000 of S&P, so both are magnified several times.
- **Expiry**: one contract every three months; before it expires you close the old one and switch to the next.
- **Nearly 24-hour trading**: Sunday evening to Friday afternoon, with a one-hour break each day.
- **No dividends**: so the ES price always differs from SPX by a little and is never exactly the same.

This index future is better suited to traders doing day trading.

  </div>
</details>

### Where the gaps in SPX come from

<details class="rc fold">
  <summary>How the gaps arise</summary>
  <div class="rc-panel rc-def">

SPX only has a value during the six and a half hours the US market is open, but the world does not stop at the close: Asian and European sessions, overnight news and earnings all play out first on ES, which trades almost around the clock. At 9:30 the next morning SPX jumps straight to where ES already is, and that leaves a gap on the chart.

  </div>
</details>
