I have a CSV of cleaned 1-minute ES futures bars (Databento's ES.c.0 continuous contract, with columns ts_event, open, high, low, close, instrument_id; timestamps in UTC). Please write Python code that finds the days with a "relentless opening selloff" and measures whether, after the selloff ends, the rest of the day turns into a trading range, a trend resumption or a reversal. Rules:
1. Convert timestamps to US Eastern time (US/Eastern) and keep RTH only, 09:30–16:15. Build 5-minute bars from the 1-minute bars, 81 bars a day.
2. A relentless opening selloff is a run of bars that starts between bar 1 and bar 10 and ends by bar 22 at the latest, and meets all of these:
   - the first bar's high is the highest high of the run, and the last bar's low is the lowest low of the run;
   - if the run is shorter than 10 bars, every bar must be a bear bar;
   - bear bars (close < open) make up at least two thirds; no bull bar's body is more than 10% of the whole drop (first bar's high − last bar's low);
   - at least 8 bars in a row (or the whole run, if it's shorter than 8) with no bull bar whose body is more than 10% of the whole drop;
   - at least three quarters of the bars have a high below the previous bar's high;
   - once the run has fallen 20% of the whole drop, no bar may close more than 20% of the whole drop above the lowest low so far, and no high may be more than 35% above it;
   - the drop within the first 20 bars is at least 60% of that year's median of "daily range ÷ open" across all days.
   When several cuts on the same day qualify, use the one that falls the most.
3. Express every distance below as a fraction of this leg: 0 is the leg's low, 1 is where the leg started (its high), and negative numbers are new lows.
4. Sort days into three groups by the close: a close more than 0.25 legs below the leg's low is "trend resumption", a close that gets back at least 80% of the leg is "reversal", and anything in between is "trading range". Print the share of each group, then the shares with cutoffs of 0 / 0.8, −0.5 / 0.8, −0.25 / 0.7 and −0.25 / 1.0. For each group, print the share that makes a new low, the medians of how much further down, the biggest bounce, the close and the leg's size, and the share where the day's low is the leg's low.
5. From the end of the leg to the close: the share that makes a new low; the shares going ≥0.25, ≥0.5 and ≥1 legs further down, and the median; how many bars after the leg ends the first new low comes (median, and the shares within 6 and 12 bars); the median biggest bounce and the shares reaching ≥0.5, ≥0.8 and ≥1 legs; among days that bounce past 80%, the shares that still close above 80% and that fall back below half; and when the day's low comes.
6. Testing the low: after the leg ends, a bounce of ≥0.3 legs followed by a return to within 0.1 legs of the low counts as a test. If it then breaks more than 0.1 legs below the low, it's "broken"; otherwise "held". Print the shares and the median close of each group.
7. What you can see 1 hour (12 bars) after the leg ends: whether there's a new low within the hour, whether the bounce reaches 0.5 legs, whether the new low goes 0.25 legs or more. Print the three-group shares for each case.
8. The next day (on the same contract, with no missing days in between): the shares closing up and down, range ÷ that year's median, the share breaking today's low, and the baseline share of next days closing up across all days.
9. For the 58 bars after the leg ends, the position of each bar's close: the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles per bar and the share "still above the low", written to JSON for the chart.
Please add comments to every step explaining why it's done that way.
