### Reflection

Before every entry, ask three questions; one "no" and I don't take the trade.

<section class="rc policy">
<div class="rc-panel rc-def">

<p class="policy-title">1. Pattern: is this wedge obvious enough?</p>

1. Without drawing lines on the 5-minute chart, can I count three pushes at a glance?
2. Are the three pushes symmetrical?
3. Is there a clear pullback between each push? The first push can be inside the trend, but it has to be obvious: for example, in a bull trend, a clear bear-bar pullback, then a new high, then another pullback, which together with the first pullback make up the three pushes.
4. After each new high, is the pullback deep? If the pullbacks are very shallow, it may instead be a small pullback trend and the trend is strong; if every new high is followed by a deep pullback and each new high is only slightly higher, the wedge works better.
5. Did the third push overshoot the trend channel line drawn from the first two pushes (or clearly exceed the prior high), and then come straight back? A failed overshoot often reverses more forcefully.
6. Are the three pushes converging? The upper and lower edges getting closer, wedge-shaped, rather than a parallel channel. A parallel channel is more likely trend continuation.

<p class="policy-title">2. Location: is it good?</p>

1. Right after a very strong trend leg (say 7–10 consecutive bars, almost all bull bars), price is usually far from the EMA: has the pullback reached the EMA yet? If it is only a two-legged pullback still far from the EMA, the entry has a low win rate. Better to wait for a third pullback and a good signal near the EMA before going long.
2. In a trading range: am I buying in the bottom third and selling in the top third?

<p class="policy-title">3. State of mind: did I "see an opportunity", or do I "want an opportunity"?</p>

1. Just opened the chart: did I spot an opportunity at a glance and want to place the order before analysing it properly?
2. Just finished a game: is my attention really back on the chart?
3. Just took a loss: my state of mind may already be affected without my noticing. Be more careful on the next one.
4. Just missed a move: am I trying to chase it back?

</div>
</section>

### Is entry time related to the result?

<TableTabs title="Entry time vs result" tabs={["Conclusion", "Is there a relationship", "Is it stable", "Other explanations", "Limitations"]}>
<div class="tt-panel">

**The later the entry, the worse the result, but it can't be called a conclusion yet.**

1. Across the 19 trades, entry time and result have a moderate negative correlation: on average, each hour later costs about 0.36R per trade.
2. Seven different tests all point the same way; dropping any single trade doesn't change the conclusion.
3. The cause looks more like the market than me: the signal bars after 12:00 aren't worse, but price doesn't go far; none of the 11 reached 2R. This is a separate problem from "rushed entries".
4. The sample is too small, and "12:00" was chosen after looking at the data, so for now it is only a hypothesis to test over the next 20 trades.

<p class="table-note">Separately, hold time and result are unrelated (ρ = −0.11, p = 0.65).</p>

</div>
<div class="tt-panel">

**First, the p-value**: suppose entry time and result are actually unrelated; how likely is it that luck alone produces a trend this clear? p = 0.03 means about 3%. By convention, below 5% counts as "doesn't look like luck".

<div class="exp-table">

| Test | Result | p |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| ① Spearman rank correlation | ρ = −0.49 | 0.03 |
| ② Linear regression | 0.36R less per trade for each hour later | 0.05 |
| ③ Bootstrap resampling | 95% interval of the slope: −0.58 to −0.11 | — |
| ④ Wins vs losses only (Mann–Whitney U) | Median entry time 11:30 for winners, 12:35 for losers | 0.055 |

</div>

1. **Spearman rank correlation**: rank the 19 trades by entry time and by result, and check whether the two rankings move together. ρ runs from −1 to +1: 0 is no relationship, −1 is a perfect "later is worse". −0.49 is moderate. It only looks at ranks, so one big winner (such as #10 at +2.85R) can't skew it. The p-value comes from permutation: shuffle the 19 results at random 100,000 times, and only about 3% of shuffles produce a relationship this strong.
2. **Linear regression**: draw the best-fitting straight line through the scatter plot; the slope is "how many R per hour later, on average". −0.36R per hour means a 10:00 entry and a 14:00 entry differ by about 1.4R on average. With a small sample the observed effect tends to be overstated, so the real gap is probably smaller.
3. **Bootstrap resampling**: draw 19 trades from these 19 with replacement, recompute the slope, repeat 10,000 times. 95% of the results fall between −0.58 and −0.11, all below zero: as long as it's this batch of trades, the "later is worse" direction is very stable. But it can't answer whether future trades will behave the same.
4. **Wins vs losses only**: ignore how much was won or lost, and only compare whether winners or losers entered earlier. Winners are earlier overall, p = 0.055, right on the 5% line.

</div>
<div class="tt-panel">

<div class="exp-table">

| Test | Result | p |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| ① Drop any one trade | ρ between −0.57 and −0.40 | — |
| ② Cap wins at +1R | ρ = −0.42 | 0.07 |
| ③ One value per day | Average per day: ρ = −0.48 | 0.07 |
| ④ Cut-point correction | Best cut-point is 11:36 | 0.14 |

</div>

1. **Drop any one trade**: remove one trade at a time and recompute, 19 times. Weakest −0.40, strongest −0.57. The conclusion doesn't rest on a single trade, such as #10 or #20.
2. **Cap wins at +1R**: treat every result above +1R as +1R, to check whether a few big winners are driving it. The relationship is still there but somewhat weaker: part of the morning's edge comes from a few big winners.
3. **One value per day**: two trades on the same day face the same market, so they aren't two independent samples. Averaging each day's trades gives 15 points for 15 days, p = 0.07; taking only the first trade of each day, p = 0.16. Fewer samples, weaker evidence.
4. **Cut-point correction**: "12:00" was picked after looking at the data. If you try every possible cut-point and keep the best, pure luck can easily produce one that looks very significant. The correction: shuffle the results 20,000 times, pick the best cut-point each time, and count how often it beats the real data. The answer is 14%. So the specific rule "no entries after a certain time" has no statistical support yet.

</div>
<div class="tt-panel">

<div class="exp-table">

| Possible cause | Test result |
|:---|:---|
| ① Worse state later in the series | Controlling for entry time, trade number has an effect ≈ 0; controlling for trade number, entry time still has −0.41 (p = 0.08) |
| ② Rushed entries | Rushing and entry time are almost unrelated; controlling for rushing, entry time still has −0.51 (p = 0.03) |
| ③ Good or bad day | Only 4 same-day early/late pairs: the later one was better twice, worse once, equal once |
| ④ Afternoon moves don't go far | Reached 2R: 3 of 8 before 12:00, 0 of 11 after 12:00 (p ≈ 0.06) |

</div>

1. **Worse state later in the series**: the later trades happen to fall mostly in the afternoon (correlation 0.61), so either one on its own looks related. A partial correlation is "how much relationship is left after removing the other factor's influence". Controlling for entry time, trade number's effect drops to zero; the other way round, entry time's effect remains. So it looks more like an "afternoon" problem than a "worse later in the series" problem.
2. **Rushed entries**: 4 of the 5 rushed trades were after 12:00, but overall rushing and entry time are barely related (ρ = 0.15). Controlling for rushing, entry time's effect is actually clearer. These are two independent problems: a rushed trade costs about −1.25R on average, each hour later about −0.3R.
3. **Good or bad day**: if it were just "some days are easy, some are hard", the early and late trades on the same day should do about equally well. Unfortunately there are only 4 pairs, not enough to tell.
4. **Afternoon moves don't go far**: the signal bars after 12:00 aren't worse (strong signal bars 8 of 11, versus 5 of 8 in the morning), but price doesn't go far: the median MFE after entry is 1.37R in the morning and 0.61R in the afternoon; there are also more TR days in the afternoon. This matches the usual pattern of lower midday volume and volatility in US stocks. The p-value is from Fisher's exact test, made for comparing two proportions in small samples.

</div>
<div class="tt-panel">

1. **Small sample**: only 19 trades. The 95% interval for ρ is −0.77 to −0.05; the true relationship could be very strong or almost nothing.
2. **Too many dimensions examined**: this review looked at entry time, hold time, weekday, setup, trade number of the day, five or six dimensions. The more you look at, the more likely you stumble on a "significant" one by chance. With the most conservative Bonferroni correction, the threshold drops from 0.05 to about 0.01, and 0.03 doesn't pass.
3. **The cut-point was chosen after the fact**: see item 4 under "Is it stable"; corrected p = 0.14.
4. **Several factors are tangled together**: the later trades are mostly in the afternoon, and the rushed trades are mostly in the afternoon too. Partial correlation removes some of it, but 19 trades can't be separated cleanly.
5. **How many trades to confirm it**: if the true relationship really is as strong as −0.49, about 30 trades would give an 80% chance of confirming it; if it's only −0.25 (small samples usually overstate the effect, so this is more realistic), about 120 trades are needed.

</div>

### Is the EMA related to the result?

<TableTabs title="EMA vs result" tabs={["Conclusion", "Is there a relationship", "Other explanations", "Limitations"]}>
<div class="tt-panel">

**There's a hint, but the evidence is weaker than for entry time.**

<div class="exp-table">

| Test | Result | p |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| ① Against both vs the rest (permutation test) | −0.35R vs +0.43R per trade, a gap of 0.78R | 0.21 |

1. **Permutation test**: shuffle the 19 results 50,000 times and count how often "pick any 5 as against both" produces a gap of 0.78R or more. About 21%, one time in five, easy to get by pure luck.
2. **Fisher's exact test**: made for comparing two proportions in small samples. 1/5 and 9/14 look very different, but 5 trades is too few, p = 0.14.
3. **Kruskal–Wallis test**: compares several groups at once to see whether they come from the same distribution. It only looks at ranks, so big winners don't distort it. p = 0.58, no systematic difference between the four combinations.
4. **Spearman rank correlation**: checks whether "number of EMAs with the trade" (0, 1, 2) rises and falls with the result. ρ = 0.21 is very weak, and the middle group is the best, so it isn't a straight line. Looking at the 5m alone (p = 0.68) or the 60m alone (p = 0.52), neither shows a significant difference either. Within reversals, 60m with vs against differ by 0.74R, p = 0.33.

### Conclusion

**Statistically it looks like a pattern, but it isn't of much practical use.** The 5 trades that didn't retest averaged +1.56R, the 6 that did +0.55R (p between 0.05 and 0.1). ### Conclusions

1. **Live, strong signal bars were no better than weak ones**: 13 strong averaged +0.13R, 6 weak +0.44R (p = 0.61), the opposite of Experiment 01 (23 strong totalling +17R, all 7 weak stopped out). Experiment 01 has a larger sample and labels checked against the definitions, so it takes precedence.
2. **Weak signal bars rarely reach 2R, and both agree on this**: 7 in the experiment plus 6 live, 13 in total, and none reached 2R; strong signal bars reached it 15 times out of 36 (42%), p = 0.004. If you take a weak signal bar, keep the target around 1R (live, weak pin bars with a 1R target give +0.53R, the best).
3. **The result of a strong pin bar depends almost entirely on the entry bar**: the 4 with good follow-through (good trend bar / acceptable follow-through) all won, +7.14R combined; the 5 with a counter bar all lost, −4.56R combined, never getting beyond 0.17–0.31R (p = 0.008). Consistent with conclusion 4 of Experiment 01.
4. **Strong trend bars did worst live**: 4 trades averaging −0.22R, none reaching 1.5R. But all 4 were after 12:00 and 2 were rushed entries; too few to conclude anything yet.
5. **Limitations**:
   1. The live signal bar labels were set at the time and not re-checked against the Experiment 01 definitions;
   2. Of the 6 weak live trades, 4 had good follow-through and all won; "weak signal bars do better" may come from the entry bar, not the signal bar itself;
   3. Live exits are discretionary and the MFE is only recorded up to the exit, so the target simulations are conservative.

<TableTabs title="Direction vs result" tabs={["Conclusion", "Is there a relationship", "Other explanations", "Limitations"]}>
<div class="tt-panel">

**Shorts clearly beat longs, but mostly because of the rushed longs and the long flags; the evidence for direction itself isn't enough.**

1. Shorts: 8 trades, 6 won, +5.49R; longs: 11 trades, 4 won, −1.16R; a gap of 0.79R per trade.
2. The difference is concentrated in two kinds of trade: long flags (6 trades, 1 won, −3.36R) and rushed longs (4 trades, all lost, −4.08R). 4 of the 5 rushed entries were longs.

4. All the long losses are in October, which was a down month overall. The problem may not be "I can't trade longs" but "going long against the month's direction".

<div class="tt-panel">

<div class="exp-table">

| Test | Result | p |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| ① Gap in average per trade (exact permutation test) | +0.69R vs −0.11R, a gap of 0.79R | 0.15 |

3. **Fisher's exact test (win rate)**: 75% and 36% look very different, but the sample is too small, p = 0.17.

1. Broad channel: 5 of 5 won, all reached 1R, +5.54R total; tight channel / spike: 1 of 7 won, −2.58R total (p between 0.015 and 0.03). This is the same thing as "is there a clear pullback between each push" in the three questions.
2. The big picture and trade direction (with / TR / against) show no relationship (p = 0.47). But when the big picture is a broad bear channel, buying reversals at the bottom won all 3, while treating it as a bull trend and buying flags lost all 3.
3. To be discounted: 3 of the tight-channel trades were rushed entries and many were in the afternoon; controlling for both at once, the difference is no longer significant. The labels may also have some hindsight in them (#8 for example), and the grouping is mine.

</div>

<div class="exp-table">

| Test | Result | p |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| ① Broad channel vs tight channel / spike (exact permutation test) | +1.11R vs −0.37R, a gap of 1.48R | 0.029 |
| ② Win rate (Fisher's exact test) | Broad channel 5 of 5 vs tight channel / spike 1 of 7 | 0.015 |
| ③ Three structures together (Kruskal–Wallis) | Ranked from best to worst: broad channel, TTR, tight channel | 0.060 |

<p class="table-note">The exact permutation test computes all 792 ways of choosing, and only 2.9% produce a bigger gap than the real one. For comparison, the big picture vs trade direction has ρ of only 0.18 (p = 0.47).</p>

### Is hold time related to the result?

<TableTabs title="Hold time vs result" tabs={["Conclusion", "Is there a relationship"]}>
<div class="tt-panel">

**No clear relationship. Hold time is mostly decided by the exit method, not a signal you can use in advance.**

1. Hold time vs result ρ = −0.14 (p = 0.58); winners and losers have similar median hold times (12.5 vs 15 minutes).
2. The big winners were mostly fast: 4 of the 5 trades above 1R finished within 15 minutes, because of the limit target. It isn't "held short so it won big" but "ran smoothly so it reached the target fast".
