> Buy gold in August 1971 and hold to October 2026: **8.6%** a year, **4.5%** after inflation. The S&P 500 (dividends reinvested) did 11.2% and 7.0% over the same period.
>
> DCA into gold every month: **7.2%** a year, 3.8% after inflation. DCA into the S&P 500: 11.7%.
>
> Gold rises in spurts. Buy at the January 1980 high and, after inflation, you only broke even in February 2025. That is 45 years.
>
> <span class="takeaway">What it's for: for long-run DCA, the S&P 500 has the better numbers. Gold's use is elsewhere. In the 12 years the S&P fell, gold rose in 8.</span>

My GC data starts in 2010. 16 years is too short, and it happens to cover one big gold bull market. This post uses the World Bank's monthly gold price instead, which starts in 1960, and takes the part after August 1971.

Why start in 1971: before that the price was set by governments. From 1934 to 1971 the US exchanged gold at $35 an ounce, and the price barely moved. In August 1971 the US said the dollar would no longer be exchanged for gold, and from then on the price moved freely.

## 1. Lump sum: 8.6% a year, 4.5% after inflation

<GoldLong part="price" />

In August 1971 gold was $43. In October 2026 it was $4,105, 95 times as much. In today's money that $43 is $356, so after inflation it grew 11.5 times.

When you buy makes a big difference:

<div class="exp-table">

| Bought in | Gold price then | Gold annualised | After inflation | S&P 500 annualised | After inflation |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| August 1971, price freed | $43 | 8.6% | 4.5% | 11.2% | 7.0% |
| January 1980, the high | $675 | 3.9% | 0.7% | 12.2% | 8.7% |
| January 2000 | $284 | 10.5% | 7.7% | 8.4% | 5.7% |
| September 2011, the high | $1,772 | 5.7% | 3.0% | 15.3% | 12.3% |
| January 2016, near the bear market low | $1,098 | 13.1% | 9.4% | 15.7% | 12.0% |

</div>

<p class="table-note">All held to October 2026. Gold prices are monthly averages.</p>

Bought at the 1980 high and held 46 years: 0.7% a year after inflation. Of the five starting points, gold beat the S&P only from 2000.

## 2. By decade: up for 10 years, flat for 20

<div class="exp-table">

| Period | Gold | S&P 500 | Inflation |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|
| August 1971–1979 | 32.7% | 5.5% | 7.9% |
| 1980s | −1.1% | 17.3% | 5.1% |
| 1990s | −3.6% | 18.0% | 2.9% |
| 2000s | 14.9% | −0.7% | 2.5% |
| 2010s | 2.7% | 13.3% | 1.8% |
| 2020–October 2026 | 16.1% | 15.6% | 4.1% |

</div>

<p class="table-note">Each cell is the annualised return over that period, using each December's monthly average.</p>

In the 1980s and 1990s gold fell for 20 years straight, while the S&P rose 17%–18% a year. The 2000s were the other way round: the S&P went nowhere for ten years and gold made 14.9% a year.

By calendar year, the S&P 500 fell in 12 years from 1972 to 2025. Gold rose in 8 of those 12, by 14.2% on average. Most of that came from 1973 and 1974, up 67% and 72%. In the 6 years the S&P fell after 2000, gold only rose by much in 2002 (20%). The other 5 were between −4% and +2%.

## 3. The worst case: a 45-year wait

In January 1980 gold averaged $675 for the month. It then fell all the way to $256 in July 1999, down 62%. The nominal price only got back to 675 in May 2006, 26 years later.

After inflation it took longer. $675 in January 1980 is $2,928 today. The low was in April 2001, down 83%. Gold only got back to that level in February 2025, the dashed line in the first chart: 45 years. Long enough for someone to start work and retire.

Over the same period the S&P 500's worst fall after inflation was 52% (August 2000 to March 2009), and it was back by May 2013, in under 13 years.

## 4. Monthly DCA: 7.2% a year

<GoldLong part="dca" />

$500 a month from August 1971 to October 2026 is 662 months and $331,000 invested. The gold is worth $3.98 million, 7.2% a year. The S&P 500 is worth $24.74 million, 11.7% a year. You can change the start and end months.

The DCA annualised return counts how long each payment was actually invested (the internal rate of return), so it can be compared directly with the lump-sum figures above.

Taking every month from August 1971 on as a starting point, and investing for 10, 20 and 30 years:

<div class="exp-table">

| Years invested | Gold median annualised | Gold worst | Starts losing money after inflation | S&P 500 median annualised | Starts where gold beat the S&P |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 years | 4.6% | −6.5% | 44.0% | 13.2% | 26.2% |
| 20 years | 6.8% | −4.0% | 40.2% | 10.9% | 25.1% |
| 30 years | 6.2% | 1.0% | 17.5% | 9.7% | 2.0% |

</div>

<p class="table-note">543, 423 and 303 starting points. Annualised returns are nominal, not adjusted for inflation.</p>

- DCA into gold for 30 years never lost money in nominal terms. After inflation, 17.5% of the starting points still lost.
- DCA into the S&P 500 for 20 years or more never lost money, even after inflation.
- For gold, DCA did slightly better than a lump sum. Over 30 years the DCA median is 6.2%, the lump-sum median 4.7%. Gold moves in spurts, so buying in instalments makes it harder to buy everything at a top.

In the last few years gold has risen faster than the S&P: 16.1% a year since 2020. Over 55 years that is rare. Investing for 10 years, gold beat the S&P from only 26% of starting points.

## 5. So should you buy gold

My conclusion: the core of DCA is still the S&P 500. Held 30 years, gold makes about 1%–4% a year after inflation, and it can leave you waiting decades, as in 1980.

Its use is that it does not move with stocks. In the years the S&P falls, gold mostly does not. Whether to put part of your money in gold, and how much, depends on the portfolio. This post does not work that out.

## 6. Checks and method

<TableTabs title="Checks and method" tabs={["Checks", "Method"]}>
<div class="tt-panel">

- I compared the World Bank monthly averages with monthly averages from my own 1-minute GC futures data, June 2010 to October 2026. The average gap is 0.23%, the largest 1.65%. Futures run slightly above spot, and the gap is the cost of carry.
- The World Bank rounds gold prices to whole dollars. Around 1971 gold was about $40, so rounding is off by at most 1.2%. Spread over 55 years of annualised return, the effect is small.
- US CPI for October 2025 was not published (government shutdown). I filled it with the geometric mean of September and November.
- From 1960 to 1971 gold was between $35 and $43. This post does not use that part. Older annual gold prices (from 1833) come from another source and are not used either.

</div>
<div class="tt-panel">

- **Gold**: monthly gold price from the World Bank commodity price data (Pink Sheet), monthly average, US$ per ounce, the 3 November 2026 release, through October 2026.
- **S&P 500**: Robert Shiller's public monthly data from Yale, monthly averages, with dividends bought back in at that month's price (total return). The same data as the DCA series.
- **Inflation**: US CPI-U (not seasonally adjusted), CPIAUCNS on FRED. "After inflation" means using CPI to convert prices into October 2026 dollars.
- **Lump-sum annualised return**: (ending price ÷ purchase price) ^ (12 ÷ months) − 1, using monthly averages at both ends.
- **DCA annualised return**: one payment each month at that month's average price. The last month only counts market value. Solve for the internal rate of return and annualise it. For after-inflation figures, each payment and the ending value are first converted with that month's CPI.
- **Every month as a starting point**: start in each month from August 1971 on, invest for a full N years, and take the value in that month.
- **By decade and by calendar year**: using each December's monthly average.
- **Not included**: fees, tax, bid-ask spreads, exchange rates. Gold ETFs charge about 0.1%–0.4% a year, and physical bars also have a buy-sell spread. Choosing ¥ just swaps $500 a month for ¥3,000. No exchange rate is applied.

</div>
