>

>

<p class="table-note">BE: once price reaches this level, the stop moves to the entry price. Stepped stop: at 1R the stop moves to the entry price, at 2R to +1R, at 3R to +2R, and so on. Rules without a breakeven level keep the stop where it started.</p>

<p class="table-note">33 of the 43 trades have no fill record. Their entry price is reconstructed as one tick beyond the signal bar. The two trades on 7/3 have no bar data, and one trade on 7/1 has a reconstructed entry that doesn't match the sheet. These three are counted at −1R, using the initial stop recorded in the sheet. Trades still open at 16:59 are counted at the close. Commissions and slippage are not included.</p>

Conclusion: a 3R target with BE at 1R is the highest, +0.42R. 2R with BE at 1R is +0.16R, and 1.5R with BE at 1R is +0.10R. What I actually did came to +0.27R, in the middle. At every target, moving to BE at 1R beats leaving the stop in place. The table comparing this with live trading is in the [live trading review](/en/posts/wedge-rep20-v2/).

## Summary

The experiment set out to answer three questions.

1. How often gold reaches 1R and 2R compared with ES. It reaches 1R less often than ES, but once past 1R it goes further: 13 of the 43 trades reached 3R.
2. Which session trades best. Early Asia is best. Shanghai and Europe roughly break even. Early Asia has only 9 trades, so I need more.
3. Whether to move to BE at 1R. Yes. Across the 13 rules, for the same target, adding BE at 1R always scores higher.

Two more things came up along the way. None of the 7 weak signal bars made money. Reversal 2nd entries are much better than 1st entries. Live trading, split by setup, points the same way: for reversals the 2nd entry is slightly higher (+0.19R against +0.09R), and for flag in trend the 1st entry is better (+0.55R against −0.24R).

Split up like this, each group of the 43 trades has only a few to a dozen or so trades, so for now I treat all of this as leads.
